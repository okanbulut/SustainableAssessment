Why this project exists

Assessment only works if the work submitted is the student's own. Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT have made this assumption harder to hold in practice. Rewriting every assessment, every term is not something university instructors can sustain.

We treat this as a sustainability problem rather than a detection problem. Instead of asking which submissions were written by AI, we ask which assessment designs stop depending on the answer.

1

Find the vulnerabilities

Real assessments from undergraduate and graduate courses, completed by ChatGPT and Gemini, then marked by the instructors who wrote them.

2

Understand the differences

Regional focus groups on how instructors read, apply and work around their institutions' AI policies and what support they say they need.

3

Hand back something usable

This toolkit: guidelines, worked examples, revision moves and policy guidance, free for any educator to use or adapt.

What we found

We ran two phases. In the first, we gave real university assessments to two frontier AI systems and scored their answers against the instructors' own keys and criteria. In the second, we interviewed faculty across five world regions about what they are doing in response.

AI performance by assessment format

Both systems performed strongly across every format we could score. The differences between formats are small, and they do not run in the direction commonly assumed: constructed-response tasks were not more resistant than multiple-choice ones.

What this means

For the formats we could score, AI performance is not the differentiator — the design of the task is. Which is why the rest of this toolkit is about design, not detection.

What instructors told us

Thirty faculty members across North America, Europe, Africa, Asia and Australia, interviewed about how their assessment practice is changing. Six patterns ran through the interviews.

Take-home written work became unverifiable

Instructors in every region described assignments, essays and problem sets that stopped functioning as evidence of learning almost overnight — problem descriptions could simply be pasted in and solved.

A reluctant return to invigilated exams

Many are reintroducing in-person, handwritten and supervised assessment. Several described it as going "full circle" after years of moving toward flexible and online formats — pragmatic rather than welcome.

A shift to oral, video and performance tasks

Oral examinations, vivas, recorded explanations and live demonstrations emerged as the preferred alternative, since students must articulate reasoning in the moment. Scalability in large courses is the open question.

Reflection and process documentation

Reflective writing, learning journals and process records were widely seen as resistant, because they require students to narrate their own experience — and were valued pedagogically in their own right.

The clearest fault line: resistance or acceptance

Some instructors design to prevent AI use; others consider that a losing battle and design to teach students to work with it well. A third group holds the question open. Region and discipline shape which view dominates.

Questioning what assessment is for

Faculty found themselves revisiting long-standing assumptions: whether exams measure what matters, which knowledge and skills are now worth developing, and what a degree certifies.

What we found about institutional policy

Policy existed at most participating universities, but it was written and implemented inconsistently — and the majority of instructors told us they wanted more specificity, particularly about when students may and may not use AI.

Instructors described the reasons for that vagueness sympathetically: AI changes too fast for fixed wording, universities are reluctant to be seen policing students, and the prior question — what knowledge and skills matter now — has not been settled. Where no policy existed, instructors were left to write their own, or to wait.

Latitude intended as academic freedom was frequently experienced as abandonment: one participant described a policy with so much latitude that it left everything to the individual instructor to decide.

Four approaches we observed in practice

01

Scale back

Restrict the format — no take-home tests, pen-and-paper only.

02

Permitted with guidance

Students may use AI, with explicit instructions on how and where.

03

Permitted with citation

Full use allowed, provided AI-generated portions are cited.

04

Open use

Assessments revised proactively on the assumption students will use AI.

These approaches co-existed not only across institutions but often within a single university — and sometimes within a single instructor's own courses.

For teaching and learning leads

What follows is what we would advise an institution to do with the policy findings above — offer instructors real choices, revisit the wording as the technology moves, and treat professional learning as a multi-year commitment rather than a workshop.

What we recommend

Give instructors a menu, not a mandate

Offer a set of sanctioned options instructors can choose between, based on their course objectives, their own readiness to work with AI, and the design of the assessment.

Review the policy annually

Revise it as the technology changes and as feedback arrives from students and instructors. A policy written once will be wrong within a year.

Plan professional learning over several years

Instructors consistently named time as the binding constraint. Make the format flexible and collaborative, and evaluate it as often as needs shift.

Six design guidelines

Held in balance deliberately: three reduce what AI can do for a student unseen, three make AI use legitimate and visible. Neither half works alone.

Resilience
Assess the process, not only the product

Grade drafts, decisions, discarded approaches and revision notes alongside the final artefact. A process record is cheap for an honest student to produce and expensive to fabricate.

Resilience
Anchor the task in local material

Data your class collected, a case discussed in week seven, a reading not available online, a site students visited. Generality is what makes a prompt easy to answer well.

Resilience
Ask students to account for their work aloud

Five minutes of structured questions on a submission tells you more than any detector. Build it in as a small graded component rather than an investigation triggered by suspicion.

Integration
Make AI use declarable — and grade the declaration

A short statement of which tools were used, for what, and what the student changed. Assess the judgement shown in that account, not merely its presence.

Integration
Put AI output on the table as material to critique

Give students a model-generated answer and ask them to find where it is wrong, shallow or unsupported. The task becomes disciplinary judgement, which is what you wanted to assess.

Integration
State the rule per task, not per course

"Permitted", "required", "not for this one" — written on the assessment itself. Most confusion we heard from instructors and students came from blanket policies applied to mixed workloads.

Redesign moves by assessment type

Pick the kind of task you're revising. Each one keeps the learning outcome intact and changes only what the task asks for.
